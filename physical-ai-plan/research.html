04 / Research project

Obstruction-triggered self-improvement.

A proposed study of imagined policy adaptation, real-world verification and active correction. One task first; claims follow evidence.

Proposed protocolOne arm · one core taskLearned predictive modelNo results implied

The question and the intended claim

Can a robot recover from an unseen obstruction by briefly fine-tuning its policy inside a learned world model, while a small, actively chosen budget of physical trials keeps the improvement grounded?

The earlier “honest self-improvement” idea supplies the correction mechanism. Obstruction-triggered adaptation supplies a concrete deployment setting. The contribution must be demonstrated against replanning with the same model and against random real-trial selection. It is not enough to show that a robot can train in imagination.

  • H1 · Adaptation: imagined policy updates improve executed recovery beyond the frozen policy.
  • H2 · Value beyond search: adaptation improves recovery or subsequent encounters beyond compute-matched world-model MPC.
  • H3 · Budgeted correction: active physical trials yield better executed success at the same trial budget than random trials.
  • H4 · Honesty: corrected predictions better discriminate successful and failed recoveries without merely predicting failure everywhere.

No result or novelty claim is assumed in this plan. Shared policy/simulator weights are an optional later factor, not a prerequisite and not proof of a self-deception mechanism.

One primary task with a real strategy change

Primary task: clear access, then grasp and place. A cobot must move a target block into a marked tray. In nominal scenes the block is directly graspable. A movable blocker prevents the usual approach in perturbed scenes, so the robot must push or reposition an object before completing the original task. Keep target, goal and reward definition fixed across paired nominal/blocked conditions.

Start in a simulator with continuous end-effector commands and a simple calibrated tabletop scene. Transfer the same observation/action contract to the available arm. Use low-dimensional state estimates for the first diagnostic; the preferred visual extension uses frozen visual features plus learned action-conditioned latent dynamics. Clearly distinguish state-based, visual and oracle observations in every result.

  • Success: the designated target is inside the marked goal region, released, and stable for a fixed observation interval within the episode time cap.
  • Blockage: the nominal approach is infeasible or has stalled under a prespecified collision/progress rule.
  • Desired behavior: an additional manipulation or a different sequence is needed; a small obstacle-avoidance detour alone is a weaker secondary condition.
  • Out of scope initially: tool invention, bimanual coordination, deformable objects, peg insertion, multiple robot embodiments and training a world foundation model from scratch.

Validate that the task has legal solutions using a diagnostic oracle or human demonstration before training. Freeze a family of blocker placements before the confirmatory experiment; do not choose only placements on which your method wins.

A feasible architecture and an upgrade path

Core route: a nominal policy + small residual actor + learned action-conditioned dynamics ensemble. A world model is the learned predictor used for imagined rollouts; the physics engine is used only for execution and ground-truth evaluation in simulation.
  1. Observation: current/history observations, robot proprioception and task goal. Infer the current model state from the actual blocked scene.
  2. Predictor: small ensemble predicting state or latent transitions. For visual work, freeze a pretrained encoder initially and learn only dynamics/readouts.
  3. Policy: a BC or existing controller with a bounded continuous residual. Use a conventional continuous actor for the first study; generative-policy adapters are optional.
  4. Reward and failure signal: geometric progress and success/contact costs for the diagnostic; a separately validated perception-based readout for hardware. A VLM judge must be audited against real labels before it becomes a reward source.
  5. Update: short imagined rollouts, a documented SAC-style synthetic-replay update, original-replay mixing, a fixed update/time cap and nominal-task retention checks.

Model choice gate, Week 3: measure training memory, rollout throughput, action sensitivity, multi-step contact prediction and compatibility with your arm. Use a compact learned model if a large checkpoint cannot pass these checks. DreamerV3 is the architecture reference; DINO-WM is a visual planning reference. DreamZero/openpi are optional scale-up routes after the core question is answered. Their installation or availability does not imply compatibility with the research loop.

The first compact implementation establishes feasibility; it does not by itself support a claim about all world-action models. A publication specifically claiming foundation-model adaptation needs an actual foundation-model experiment.

The complete adaptation and correction loop

  1. Observe the failure. Use recent actual observations to detect stagnation, failed progress or an obstructed action. Save the failure state and base checkpoint.
  2. Ground the imagined scene. Re-encode the new observation/history. A new obstacle may need only a new inferred state; model-weight updates are warranted when predictions remain wrong, not automatically whenever a scene changes.
  3. Dream locally. Freeze the predictor within the round. Roll out short action sequences from real anchor states and nearby development states; use ensemble variation to expose uncertainty.
  4. Fine-tune the policy. Update the residual actor and critics with synthetic experience and retained replay. Save the adapted checkpoint so the change persists after executing one trajectory.
  5. Choose real verification trials. Rank legal development candidates using an initial score such as predicted success × ensemble outcome disagreement. Apply a diversity filter. Compare with uniform random selection from exactly the same pool.
  6. Execute and account. Record actual outcomes and transitions, aborted attempts, robot time, reset time, human time and all compute. No candidate's actual outcome is available at selection time.
  7. Correct and repeat. Add selected physical data to a correction buffer, update the predictor between rounds, then repeat imagined policy adaptation from a specified checkpoint rule.
  8. Evaluate separately. Use untouched layouts for confirmatory evaluation. Keep the original nominal-task suite to measure retention. Rollback/time-cap events remain in the results.

Checkpoint rule: for a clean method comparison, every condition starts from identical base policy/model/data. Within each condition, budget points are nested along one declared correction trajectory. Do not compare one method reset to the base with another allowed to accumulate earlier updates.

Optional diagnostics: exact-physics planning as an oracle; equal-budget real-only fine-tuning; uncertainty-only acquisition; no residual bound; short versus long imagination. Add only after the primary five-method comparison is complete.

Match observations, initial data, model architecture, task objective, real-trial allowance and evaluation conditions. Report both model-call-matched and wall-clock-aware comparisons where practical: an actor update and an MPC rollout have different compute costs. A baseline may use its allotted compute for better search instead of gradient updates.

Data splits, interventions and leakage prevention

  • Training: nominal demonstrations, legal play and failures in known layouts. Record the distribution of contact and blocked states.
  • Development/acquisition: separate layouts used for model debugging, score tuning, hyperparameters and legal correction candidate generation.
  • Held-out evaluation: unseen blocker positions/orientations, fixed before the final run. No training, candidate selection, stopping-rule tuning or threshold tuning on these layouts.
  • Harder generalization: new shapes, friction or missing visual evidence, labeled separately rather than pooled into one generalization score.
  • Retention: nominal tasks evaluated after adaptation to detect forgetting or a policy that only works near the current failure state.

Keep whole episodes and layout families together. Splitting adjacent video frames across train and test leaks information. Match initial states and actions for prediction-versus-execution comparisons. If correcting a held-out deployment scene is itself part of the test setting, explicitly separate its adaptation trials from later evaluation trials and report both costs; do not silently change the no-test-training protocol.

Save a manifest for every trial: split/layout ID, base and adapted policy hashes, model version, acquisition score before execution, generator seed, commanded/executed actions, observed outcome, adaptation duration and human/reset time. Keep outcome labels blind to the method where feasible.

Budgets that fit the three-month window

Simulation first. Start with three training seeds, 24 held-out layouts and three execution repeats per method at the headline budget: 5 × 3 × 24 × 3 = 1,080 evaluation episodes. Add intermediate budget evaluations only after measuring runtime. Correction budgets of 0/5/15/30 episodes are a proposed starting grid; fix them using development feasibility, before final outcomes are seen.

At the 30-episode cap, two correction methods × three seeds × 30 = 180 executed simulator correction episodes. Nested budgets reuse previous correction data; evaluation repeats do not count as free training data. These are proposed allocations, not completed experiments or a power calculation.

At an observed 1.5–3 minutes per episode including resets, this is approximately 8–22 hours of episode operation, before calibration, troubleshooting, annotation and training. Measure these overheads in Week 2; reserve larger project blocks accordingly. Small hardware studies support preliminary transfer evidence, not strong multi-task reliability claims. If data needs exceed the budget, narrow the claim or retain simulation as the main study.

Compute budget: profile one predictor rollout, one actor update and one complete adaptation round. Initially explore development time caps such as 30 seconds, 2 minutes and 10 minutes; choose the confirmatory cap from deployment needs and actual throughput. Record GPU model, peak memory, wall time and model calls. These time caps are targets to test, not promised achievable latencies.

Metrics, figures and what would falsify the idea

  1. Executed recovery success: fraction completing the same goal after blockage, with denominators and per-layout results.
  2. Success versus real correction trials: active and random at matched nested budgets; include uncertainty and the zero-correction point.
  3. End-to-end recovery latency: blockage detection, re-encoding, imagination, gradient updates, verification and execution; report physical resets separately.
  4. Prediction honesty: predicted success minus observed success, Brier score when predictions are probabilistic, and success/failure discrimination. A pessimistic model can have a small optimism gap and still be useless.
  5. Retention and reuse: nominal-task performance and success on subsequent obstruction encounters; show whether repeated use amortizes training time.
  6. Resource costs: unique real episodes, transitions, robot minutes, operator/reset time, gradient steps, predictor calls and GPU time.

Use paired layout-level bootstrap intervals for method differences, and report variation across independent training seeds separately. Bootstrap intervals cannot repair an unrepresentative test set or systematic labeling error. Hardware sample sizes may be too small for tight intervals; show raw outcomes.

Falsification: if compute-matched MPC matches or beats adaptation, the policy update has no demonstrated advantage for this task. If active selection does not beat random, do not claim superior robot-hour efficiency. If imagined gains fail to transfer, the main finding may be a model limitation rather than a successful adaptation method. If only state-based results work, do not claim visual world-model generalization.

Decision gates and scope control

  • Week 2: reset, act, log and replay work. Simplify integration if this fails.
  • Week 4: action-sensitive predictions and a same-model planner run. Improve model/data before imaginary optimization if blockage is not represented.
  • Week 6: the complete update/correction loop executes. Select the main claim from development evidence, then freeze the confirmatory protocol.
  • Week 8: the core comparison is repeatable. Diagnose active-selection or adaptation failures before adding more tasks.
  • Week 9: claims have direct baseline/ablation support. A second task or foundation-model adapter is optional.
  • Week 11: a clean reproduction, figures, raw outcomes and limitations are available.

Change tasks or thresholds only for documented development reasons, with a new protocol version. Do not make the scene harder solely to obtain a desired imagined-real gap, or discard layouts because a baseline solves them.

The artifact and the publication route

  • A runnable training/adaptation/evaluation recipe with pinned dependencies and a tiny smoke configuration.
  • Dataset/split manifests, selected paired imagined/executed examples and complete cost ledgers.
  • Versioned checkpoints or exact provenance and instructions when redistribution is restricted.
  • One headline budget curve, a planning-versus-adaptation comparison, retention results and a failure gallery.
  • A two-minute narrated demo; a 10-minute technical presentation; an evidence-backed technical report.
  • A preprint or submission-ready paper only when the evidence, authorship and permissions support the claims.

Potential audiences include robot learning, model-based adaptation, embodied evaluation and data-efficient manipulation. Choose a venue after the result is clear and verify actual submission dates; this plan does not assume any 2027 deadline. A useful reproducible negative-result artifact is preferable to an unsupported success claim.
