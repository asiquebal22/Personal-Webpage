Enhanced SpO2 Estimation Using Explainable Machine Learning and Neck Photoplethysmography

A wearable pillow reads blood oxygen from your neck. The catch: everyone's skin, neck, and sensor settings are different. This paper builds a system that automatically figures out which device settings work for which people — and explains its own reasoning.

Zhong, Jatav, Afrin, Shivaram, Bukkapatnam — Texas A&M University

Why SpO2, and why at the neck?

SpO2 is the percentage of your hemoglobin that's carrying oxygen. Healthy adults sit above ~95%; sustained drops can signal serious problems.

Finger/earlobe pulse oximeters (transmission-based) are accurate but awkward for continuous, long-term, at-home wear. Reflectance sensors can be placed almost anywhere — like a pillow around your neck — but they're more sensitive to skin tone, hair, fat deposits, and sensor settings. That trade-off is the entire motivation for this paper.

The core problem: one-size-fits-all doesn't fit

A single "universal" SpO2 model trained on average data tends to fail on people whose signals look different — different skin color, neck fat, hair density, or even a different sensor brightness setting. Prior work confirmed this:

  • Classification-based SpO2 models on 610 patients overfit and generalized poorly.
  • Regression models on finger/wrist/forehead PPG (28 patients) had test MAE ranging 1.81%–3.18% — wildly different across patients.
  • Skin tone, obesity, age, and gender change the optical properties of skin/vessels, directly corrupting PPG signals.

Instead of throwing out "bad" signals or forcing one model on everyone, this paper asks a different question:

"For each device setting, which subgroup of people does it actually work well for — and can a machine explain why?"

The general framework

The proposed solution is a 3-phase framework for building personalized wearables, applicable beyond just SpO2:

🧪 Training Phase
Cluster users by biometrics + signals; train a model per cluster per setting
→
✅ Operation Phase
New user is matched to a cluster via inclusion/exclusion rules → gets the right setting + model
→
🔁 Altering Phase
Periodic recalibration vs. gold standard; XAI + domain experts refine the criteria over time

What are "inclusion-exclusion criteria"?

Simple rule chains like:

IF approximate_entropy > 0.42
  AND autocorrelation_variance < 0.15
    THEN → include in Cluster A → use "Brachiocephalic-IR-15" setting

They're built automatically from clustering + SHAP, instead of being hand-designed by a clinician.

The device: "SleepEaze"

What it is

A patent-pending pillow-based wearable with a MAX30105 reflectance sensor embedded in the neck-support area, plus a therapeutic massager. It measures green, red, and IR LED reflectance over the brachiocephalic and carotid arteries — non-standard sites, unlike a finger clip.

Experiment scale

  • 33 human subjects
  • 80 sensor settings per subject (5-factor full factorial: 2 locations × 2 LED types × 5 brightness levels × 2 sampling rates × 2 pulse widths)
  • Ground truth SpO2 from a Contec CMS50E finger oximeter (range 90–100%)
  • IRB-approved, Texas A&M, data collected over 1 year

794 → ~240–400 features

Using the tsfresh library, each signal chunk is converted into features capturing:

  • Energy-based: FFT coefficients, CWT coefficients, signal energy
  • Entropy-based: approximate entropy
  • Dynamic-systems-based: autoregressive coefficients, partial autocorrelation
  • Statistical: min, max, mean, variance

Plus 13 biometric dummy variables: gender, sitting/standing, neck fat deposits, age bracket, skin color, hair density. These help form clusters but are removed before the SpO2 regression itself, so predictions stay independent of them.

Finding natural subgroups: K-means clustering

For every one of the 80 settings, subjects are grouped by K-means using their signal + biometric features. To pick the number of clusters K, three standard indicators are used:

Silhouette score

Higher = better separated clusters. Paper's average was maximized at K=2.

Calinski–Harabasz (CH)

Higher = better. Also maximized at K=2.

Davies–Bouldin (DB)

Lower = better. Minimized at K=20 — but that means tiny, impractical clusters.

Curves are illustrative reconstructions matching the qualitative trends reported in Section 5.3 / Figure 7 (Silhouette & CH maximized at K=2; DB index minimized at K=20). Exact plotted values were not published in the extracted text, so this recreates the shape, not the raw numbers.

Despite K=20 minimizing DB index, the authors chose K=2 — the statistically-favored choice by two of three metrics, and practically important because it keeps the largest cluster big enough (18–32 subjects) to actually train and validate a regression model on. This is a good example of statistical optimality vs. practical usability.

Opening the black box: SHAP

K-means clusters and random forest predictions are both "black boxes" — they group/predict, but don't say why. The paper uses SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) to answer "why," which is also used to derive the human-readable inclusion-exclusion criteria.

Build the intuition: the Shapley value game

SHAP comes from cooperative game theory. Imagine 3 "features" (players) that jointly produce a prediction (payoff). The Shapley value fairly splits credit for the outcome across every possible order in which features could "join." Play with the sliders — the bars always sum to the total value, exactly like Eq. In the real paper this same math is applied — via the fast Tree-SHAP algorithm — to a random forest's actual predictions and to a surrogate classifier standing in for K-means clusters.

How do you SHAP an unsupervised clustering model?

SHAP needs a supervised model. Since K-means has no "prediction function" to explain, the authors trained a surrogate classifier (a balanced random forest) to distinguish the largest cluster (234 samples / 18 subjects) from the smaller one (195 samples / 15 subjects) — using the same 248 features. It reached 100% train and test accuracy, meaning it faithfully mimicked the clustering decision, so it's safe to explain that classifier with SHAP instead.

Takeaways

What worked

  • Automated, no-manual-labeling subgroup discovery via clustering
  • SHAP made both the clusters and the regression model interpretable
  • Signal features mattered more than biometrics for the best setting
  • 16% RMSE / 14–18% MAE improvement over baselines

Limitations & future work

  • Only the largest cluster per setting was deeply analyzed — smaller clusters weren't fully evaluated
  • Exclusion from one setting doesn't mean exclusion from the device — another setting may suit that user
  • Feature interaction effects and extension to other wearable sensors are open directions

"The primary purpose of the criteria is not to exclude users from the device, but to match prospective users with the settings and prediction models that will actually work for them."
