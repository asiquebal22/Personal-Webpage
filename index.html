Research Summary

My research focuses on developing generalizable AI and machine learning methods for scientific discovery — including generative modeling (flow matching, diffusion), post-training via reinforcement learning (PPO, DPO), model interpretability, causal inference, and physics-informed models — applied to health care, materials science, and complex physical systems under uncertainty.

Fundamental and applied work on these topics appears in venues such as NAACL, IEEE TPAMI, Communications Physics (Nature Portfolio), IEEE Transactions on Signal Processing, and JAIR, and has earned finalist or winner recognition in eight best-paper competitions across IEEE, INFORMS, and IISE. This research has been supported by over $11M from NSF, NIH, DOD, and industry partners.

Research

Machine learning has been most successful where data is abundant and the question is prediction. Scientific and engineering problems are usually neither: experiments are expensive, observations are noisy, and what we want from a model is not a forecast but an explanation — which physics governs this system, which variable caused this failure, which molecule is worth synthesizing. My group develops methods that recover interpretable structure under these conditions, with guarantees that survive contact with real instruments and real decisions.

Scientific and engineering decisions are constrained less by data volume than by the cost of each observation and the difficulty of extracting mechanism from noise. My group develops methods that recover interpretable structure — physical laws, causal relationships, and the value of the next experiment — from limited, uncertain data.

Discovering governing equations

Existing equation-discovery methods model observed data as corrupted solely by measurement noise, treating the unknown coefficients of a dynamical system as fixed quantities. Real systems violate this premise: viscosity and density vary across fluids, feedstock and process parameters vary across manufacturing runs, and the observed dynamics reflect not just sensor corruption but genuine physical uncertainty. We introduce a stochastic inverse physics-discovery framework that re-envisions the unknown coefficients as random variables, inferring their posterior distributions by matching the push-forward of posterior samples to the empirical data distribution.

Major finding: accounting for system variability alongside measurement noise is the key to consistently recovering the correct governing equations. Across ecological dynamics, chaotic attractors, oscillatory systems, and experimental capillary infiltration, the method recovers the true set of active physics terms without spurious inclusions — where competing approaches either introduce higher-order terms that misrepresent the physics or omit essential ones entirely. Because uncertainty is attributed to both sources rather than absorbed into the noise estimate, the resulting credible intervals remain calibrated instead of artificially narrowing.

Causal inference

Observational data is compatible with many causal structures, which makes identifying the source of an anomaly ill-posed without additional assumptions. Existing methods buy identifiability by restricting the function class, assuming non-Gaussian noise, fitting models in both directions, or requiring a known dependency graph. We look instead for sources of asymmetry that require none of these. Our central observation is that when a system shifts from a normal to a faulty state, transporting samples between the two distributions defines a velocity field, learned via flow matching. The root cause is independent of the causal mechanism, so its velocity is unconstrained; downstream variables are mediated by the mechanism, so their velocities are stretched and compressed by its Jacobian. Causation leaves a signature in how a variable moves, not merely in where it ends up.

Major finding: the velocity field factorizes in the causal direction and provably cannot factorize in reverse for any nonlinear mechanism — an identifiability result requiring no causal graph, no bidirectional model fitting, and no assumptions on the noise distribution. On a cloud microservice benchmark it ranks the true root cause among top candidates more reliably than graph-based baselines, while being the only method that operates without knowledge of the service dependency graph.

A parallel thread asks a related question in clinical settings: what minimal, plausible change to a patient's profile would flip a model's prediction? A counterfactual is only useful if it is sparse (few features altered, so the causal factor is isolated), plausible (immutable attributes untouched), diverse (revealing distinct explanations rather than variations on one), and realistic (drawn from the actual data distribution rather than its tails). SCGAN achieves all four by generating counterfactuals adversarially — with dropout training of the discriminator to promote sparsity, an explicit diversity penalty, and feature masking to protect immutable attributes.

Major finding: optimization-based counterfactual methods become computationally prohibitive in high dimensions and tend to produce instances lying in the tails of the data distribution — technically valid, clinically implausible. Generating from the learned distribution instead yields explanations that are both realistic and actionable. Applied to pre-operative breast MRI, the method isolates a small set of imaging and molecular features causally linked to treatment response, pointing toward decisions an oncologist can actually act on.

Active learning

When each experiment consumes material, instrument time, or a patient's participation, the governing question is what to measure next. Acquisition functions in the literature carry an implicit bias: some spread queries uniformly across unexplored space, others concentrate near sharp changes in the response. Methods that attempt both typically fix the balance in advance or decay it on a preset schedule. We developed BHEEM, a Bayesian hierarchical approach that treats the exploration–exploitation trade-off as a random variable and updates its posterior as data accumulates — capturing both the noise within each query stage and the shifting demands of the function across stages. Since the likelihood is intractable, we formulate an approximate Bayesian computation scheme based on the linear dependence of candidate queries in feature space.

Major finding: no single trade-off value works well across functions, or even across iterations of the same function — which is precisely why fixing it in advance underperforms. By inferring the balance dynamically, the method converges in fewer queries than either pure strategy and never gets trapped in one mode. Demonstrated on simulated benchmarks and on predicting lattice constants of MAX-phase materials from composition and elastic properties.

Generative models are increasingly capable of proposing candidates — molecules, hypotheses, microstructures — but proposing is not the hard part. The hard part is ensuring the proposals are physically realizable, chemically synthesizable, and worth the cost of testing. That constraint, rather than raw generative capacity, is where the methodological work lies.

Molecular and materials discovery In progress

Trial-and-error search over chemical space is infeasible given the number of possible molecular structures. We use goal-directed generative models — graph-based reinforcement learning, and more recently adjoint matching applied to pretrained molecular foundation models in data-scarce regimes — to propose candidate structures optimized against multiple target properties. A representative problem is identifying functional groups that impart self-healing behavior to a host polymer through reversible interactions, without degrading its mechanical strength or exceeding what is synthetically attainable.

Emerging finding: property-driven rewards successfully steer generation into regions of chemical space the base model does not reach on its own. But optimizing a property in isolation invites the model to exploit loopholes — it satisfies the objective by constructing strained, unstable ring systems that would never survive synthesis or attach stably to a polymer backbone. Constraining generation toward physically realizable chemistry is therefore not a postprocessing step but part of the objective itself.

Hypothesis generation for materials design

Before a candidate can be synthesized, someone has to propose it — and the space of plausible hypotheses is far larger than any group can explore. We developed goal-driven, constraint-guided LLM agents that generate materials hypotheses under explicit design objectives and feasibility constraints, rather than producing unconstrained speculation.

Major finding: the value of an LLM agent in this setting comes not from its generative fluency but from how tightly its search is bounded. Agents given explicit goals and constraints produce hypotheses that are substantively more useful than free-form generation, which tends toward the plausible-sounding but unactionable.

Emulating microstructural evolution

Phase-field simulation of microstructural evolution is accurate but far too slow to place inside a design loop. We build data-driven emulators that reproduce this evolution by first finding a low-dimensional tensor decomposition of the microstructure, then learning its dynamics in that reduced space — using nonlinear regression, or tensor-decomposed convolutional and recurrent architectures where longer-range temporal structure matters. Applied to spinodal decomposition and phase separation, and extended to predicting electromigration-mediated damage in polycrystalline interconnects.

Major finding: microstructural evolution is far lower-dimensional than its pixel-level representation suggests. Once the right reduced coordinates are identified, the dynamics become learnable from modest simulation data — and the emulator generalizes across conditions rather than memorizing trajectories, which is what makes it usable for design rather than merely for replay.

Methods earn trust by surviving contact with real instruments, real bandwidth limits, and real production constraints. This thread covers foundational work and applications that both motivate and stress-test the methodological research above.

Transformers for geometric and sequential reasoning

Attention-based architectures are well suited to problems where the relationship between distant elements matters more than local structure. We apply them to translating orthographic freehand sketches into 3D CAD models — a task requiring the model to reason jointly across multiple views, infer the geometric primitives a designer intended, and produce output that is parametrically editable rather than merely visually similar. Related work uses learned sequence models to emulate thermal fields in additive manufacturing, replacing expensive finite-element solves inside the design loop.

Why it matters: a generated mesh is a dead end for a designer; a generated primitive sequence is something they can modify. Framing CAD reconstruction as structured generation rather than surface fitting is what makes the output usable downstream.

Unsupervised learning

Labels are scarce or absent in most scientific measurement settings, which makes unsupervised structure discovery foundational rather than optional. We established consistency guarantees for the max-flow problem as a route to unsupervised image segmentation — showing that a graph-cut formulation, properly estimated, converges to the correct partition without supervision. Related work detects change in complex dynamical systems using intrinsic phase and amplitude synchronization, and learns acoustic emission signatures from nanoindentation-based lithography to characterize microstructure directly from the sound a process makes.

Why it matters: segmentation and change detection methods are usually evaluated empirically and adopted on faith. Establishing statistical consistency changes what can be claimed about a result — and matters most precisely in the scientific settings where no ground-truth labels exist to fall back on.

Digital twins under bandwidth and hardware constraints

A digital twin is only as good as the sensor data reaching it — and in practice that data must cross limited network bandwidth and be processed on constrained edge hardware. Naive downsampling discards exactly the transients that matter for diagnosis. We develop vector quantization approaches that learn a compact discrete codebook for complex sensor streams, preserving diagnostically relevant structure at a fraction of the transmitted volume. Related work uses complex-valued autoencoders for machinery audio, operating on the full time–frequency representation rather than discarding phase.

Why it matters: compression for digital twins is not a generic signal-processing problem. What must be preserved is defined by the downstream diagnostic task, which means the representation should be learned against that task rather than against reconstruction error alone.
